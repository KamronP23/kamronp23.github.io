Burned Out, or Just Tired? How to Tell the Difference

By Kamron Poosti, LMFT · November 12, 2026

The short version: tiredness improves when you rest. Burnout does not. If you have taken a weekend, or a week, or a whole vacation, and come back feeling roughly the same as when you left, that is the clearest signal that what you are dealing with is not a sleep deficit.

What is the actual difference?

Tiredness is a deficit. You are short of rest, and rest repays it. The arithmetic works.

Burnout is not a deficit, it is a state. It builds over months of effort that does not resolve, usually in a situation where you have little control and the demands do not stop. Rest does not clear it because rest was never the thing missing. People describe arriving back from time off and feeling the dread return somewhere between the airport and the first email.

The practical test is the one in the first paragraph. Take the rest, then notice what happened. If a genuine break moved the needle, you were tired. If it did not, something else is going on.

What does burnout look like beyond being tired?

Exhaustion is only one part of it, and on its own it is the least distinctive part. Two others tend to come with it.

Cynicism, or a kind of distance. Work you used to care about starts to feel pointless. You find yourself irritated by colleagues, or by clients, or by people who are still enthusiastic. Some people describe going flat rather than going angry, as though someone turned the color down.

And a drop in your sense of competence. Tasks you used to do easily now take longer and feel harder, and you start to suspect you are worse at your job than you were. That is usually the state talking rather than an accurate assessment, but it is extremely convincing from the inside.

When all three are present together, that pattern is more informative than exhaustion alone.

Why does rest not fix it?

Because the thing generating it is still running when you get back.

If the load is unsustainable, a week away pauses it rather than changing it. If you have no say over how the work happens, that is still true on the Monday. And if the exhaustion has started to erode your confidence, time off does not rebuild that, because confidence comes back from doing work that goes well, not from being away from work.

This is why “just take a break” so often fails and then makes people feel worse. The break was not wrong, it was just answering a question nobody asked.

Could it be something else?

Yes, and this part matters more than the rest of the article.

Burnout overlaps heavily with depression, and the two can be hard to tell apart from the inside. The rough distinction is that burnout usually stays attached to the work, while depression tends to spread across everything, including things you would ordinarily enjoy for their own sake. But that line is blurry, and plenty of people have both.

Several physical conditions also produce the same picture: thyroid problems, anemia, sleep apnea, vitamin deficiencies, and the aftermath of a viral illness. If this has been going on for months, it is worth getting bloodwork and a conversation with your doctor rather than assuming burnout is the whole explanation. That is not a hedge. People spend years treating a medical problem as a motivation problem.

What helps?

Being specific about what is actually generating the load. “Work is too much” is not actionable. “Three of my meetings have no decision-maker in them and I am the fallback for two other teams” is. Getting from the first to the second is most of the work.

Looking at control as well as volume. Burnout tracks how little say you have at least as much as how much you are doing. Two people working the same hours can land in completely different places depending on how much of it they chose.

Rebuilding the competence piece deliberately, rather than waiting for it to return. It usually comes back through small pieces of work that go well, which means the instinct to take on something big to prove you have still got it tends to backfire.

And separating what you can change at work from what you cannot, then deciding honestly what you want to do about the part you cannot. Sometimes that conversation is the whole thing.

When is it worth talking to someone?

If a real break did not help, if it has been going on for months, if it has started to reach outside work, or if you are making decisions about your career from inside the exhaustion, those are the ordinary reasons people come in.
